1. The Verdict in One Page

Start with what is real. This is a technically well-built site. The pages are served as finished HTML from a fast global network, they load without a single console error, they do not break on a phone screen, and search engines receive exactly the same content a human does. The 611 published articles across four languages represent genuine work, and the translation quality is high. None of that is in question.

The problem is that the site does not currently do the job you are using it for. It is a sales resource, and a sales resource has to do two things: convince a stranger, and let that stranger reach you. Right now it does neither reliably. Five compounding problems explain almost all of the 90 findings in this report.

  1. There is no working way to become a customer. The only lead-capture form on the site is switched off in the code and silently throws away every email typed into it. There is no contact page, no email address, and no phone number anywhere. On the page that sells compliance services to financial institutions, the button marked "Contact Enterprise Sales" links to the page it already sits on.
  2. Almost the whole site is a dead end. All 607 blog posts contain exactly zero links into the pages that sell. Not few: zero. Your token calculator, the single most useful thing you own, receives four inbound links from the entire site. A reader who arrives from Google finishes the article and has nowhere to go.
  3. Key pages do not contain what their titles promise. The navigation item "Compare Models" leads to a page with no comparison table on it at all, and its only pricing element sends the visitor to a different company's website. The Claude pricing reference shows prices that are roughly two model generations out of date.
  4. Machines cannot read the site properly. There is no structured data on any of the 656 pages, no machine-readable publication date anywhere, and 1,511 of the 3,275 language annotations point at pages that do not exist. For a site whose value is current pricing data, being unable to prove freshness is a direct competitive loss.
  5. Nothing proves you are a real company. There is no About page, no company name, no address, no privacy policy and no terms. The social icons in the footer are unedited placeholders. A cautious buyer performing basic diligence finds nothing and leaves.

The sequencing logic. These problems multiply rather than add. Fixing search visibility while the site cannot capture a lead simply delivers more strangers to a dead end. Publishing more articles while no article links anywhere deepens the same hole. So the order below is deliberate: first make it possible to convert a visitor at all, then make the existing traffic flow toward those conversion points, and only then work on bringing more people in.

How to read this report.

Each chapter covers one root cause, not one symptom. It shows the evidence, explains the business consequence in plain language, and ends with a box stating the takeaway and the fix. Severity labels are Critical, High, Medium and Low. Chapter 10 turns everything into a dated action plan with an owner per task. Chapter 11 records what is genuinely working, and Chapter 12 lists every claim we could not verify.

Findings by severity

Why the language links fail, by locale

The site tells search engines which page is the Spanish, Indonesian or Vietnamese version of each article. Those instructions are generated by swapping the language code in the web address. That works for the 11 standard pages in each language, whose addresses match across languages. It fails for every article, because article addresses are translated. The pattern is almost identical in all four languages, which confirms a single mechanical cause rather than scattered mistakes.

3. A Visitor Who Wants to Buy Cannot Reach You

Critical  This is the most consequential finding in the audit, and it is also among the cheapest to fix.

The site has exactly two forms. Both are newsletter sign-ups, one on the home page and one on the blog index. Both are disabled in the source code. The form tag carries the instruction onsubmit="return false;", the email box has no name, and the form has no destination address. There is no newsletter service connected anywhere in the codebase.

The practical effect: a visitor types their email, presses Subscribe, and the page does nothing. No confirmation, no error, no network request. The address is discarded. This is true on the home page and the blog index, in all four languages.

Beyond the form, there is no other route to you. Across every page we fetched there are zero email links and zero telephone links. There is no contact page: /en/contact/, /en/about/, /en/legal/ and /en/imprint/ all return "page not found". The enterprise compliance page, which pitches services to banks and listed companies, carries two prominent calls to action that both point back at the page the visitor is already reading.

What this means.

Every visitor the site attracts today, from any language and any source, has no functioning way to identify themselves as interested. Money spent on traffic before this is fixed is money spent delivering people to a closed door. The fix is roughly a day of work: connect the two forms to a real mailing service, build one contact page with an email address and a form that actually posts, and correct the two compliance buttons to point at it.

4. Ninety-Three Percent of the Site Leads Nowhere

Critical  Of the 656 published pages, 607 are blog articles. That is 93 percent of the site. We examined the article body of every one of them and counted the links pointing to other pages on the site.

The total is zero. Not an average of zero, not a low number: across all 607 articles in four languages, the body text contains no internal links whatsoever. The only links inside an article element are the two social sharing buttons at the foot of the page.

The consequence shows up on the pages that matter. The table below counts how many pages anywhere on the site link to each English money page.

The token calculator is the most genuinely useful asset on the site and the most likely reason someone would remember the brand. It has 480 words on it and four links pointing to it. The AI trends page has none at all.

There is a second, compounding effect. Because no article links to another article, search engines see 607 isolated pages rather than a connected body of work on one subject. That is a large part of why the site does not rank as an authority on a topic it has written about 607 times.

What this means.

You have already paid for the hardest part, which is having 607 pages of content that can attract strangers. None of that traffic is being routed anywhere. Adding two or three contextual links per article, pointing at the calculator and the relevant provider page, converts an existing cost into an existing asset. This does not require new content, and for the highest-traffic articles it can be done by hand in a few days.

5. The Pages Promise Answers They Do Not Contain

Critical  A visitor arrives expecting a specific answer. On several key pages the answer is not there.

The comparison page contains no comparison

The main navigation offers "Compare Models". It leads to a page titled "AI Model Type Overview". We counted the comparison tables on that page: there are none. There are no prices on it either. What it contains is three category cards and a banner advertising live pricing, whose button sends the visitor to aitokenking.com.tw, a different website.

The pricing reference is about two model generations out of date

The Claude page carries a section headed "Pricing Reference". It lists Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Claude 3.5 Haiku and Claude 3 Opus. Those models have been superseded. The site's own article published on 30 July 2026 states that the current Opus model costs 5 dollars per million input tokens and 25 dollars per million output tokens. The reference page still shows the older Opus at 15 and 75 dollars, which is three times and three times again the current figures.

Two articles featured on the home page describe the product incorrectly

The home page features seven articles. Two of them explain AI tokens as though they were cryptocurrency assets on a blockchain. One states that the cost of a token includes "the network fees associated with transferring it". Another says that "by leveraging blockchain technology, AI-powered systems can create secure and tamper-proof tokens".

This conflates two unrelated ideas. An AI token is a fragment of text that a language model processes. It is not an asset, it cannot be transferred, and there are no network fees. A technical buyer, who is precisely the audience for an API cost resource, will recognise this immediately.

Risk.

Incorrect pricing presented as a reference is the most damaging error a pricing resource can make, because accuracy is the entire value proposition. A visitor who checks one figure against the provider's own website and finds it wrong has no reason to trust any other number on the site.

What this means.

Three fixes, in order of urgency. Correct the Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini pricing blocks and add a visible "last checked" date to each. Either build the comparison table the navigation promises, or rename the navigation item to match what the page actually is. Then rewrite or remove the two blockchain articles and drop them from the home page feature list today.

6. Search Engines and AI Assistants Cannot Read the Site Properly

The good news first: the site is served as complete HTML, and we confirmed that Google's crawler, OpenAI's crawler and an ordinary browser all receive byte-for-byte identical pages. Many sites fail here. This one does not. What is missing is the machine-readable layer on top.

No structured data anywhere

Critical  All 656 pages carry zero structured data, the standardised summary telling a search engine what a page is. No organisation identity, no article records, no breadcrumbs, and no question-and-answer markup, despite the home, calculator and comparison pages already displaying 8 to 14 qualifying question blocks.

No publication or update dates that a machine can read

Critical  No page carries a machine-readable date: no published-time tag, no date element, no sitemap date. The only signal is the server timestamp, which reports the last rebuild rather than when the content was written.

For most sites this is minor. Here it is central: the product is current pricing information, and nothing lets Google, or an AI assistant deciding what to cite, establish that it is recent.

Language links that point at pages which do not exist

Critical  Of 3,275 language annotations, 1,511 point to addresses returning "page not found". We requested all 1,257 unique targets individually and every one failed. In total 603 of 656 pages, or 91.9 percent, emit at least one such dead link.

The cause is one line of template logic: the alternate address is produced by swapping the language code in the current address. Standard pages survive because their addresses match in every language. Articles do not, because theirs are translated. The English article at /en/blog/ai-token-basics-for-beginners/ claims a Spanish version at the same path, which does not exist. The real one is /es/blog/ai-token-explained-guia-para-principiantes/.

The website answers on two different addresses

High  The site is served from www.aitoken.global, but every canonical tag, social sharing address, language link and all 656 sitemap entries name the address without www, which redirects. The site's own instructions to Google point at a doorway rather than a room. The redirect is also temporary rather than permanent, and adds 217 milliseconds.

Already in progress.

An open pull request on the branch antonioduran/canonical-www-seo-fix resolves this address problem, and also corrects a wrong fallback domain in the configuration. It is worth merging. It does not fix the broken language links: it moves those 1,511 dead addresses to the new hostname, where they remain dead. That needs a separate change.

What this means.

The site does the expensive part correctly and the cheap part not at all. Structured data, real dates, and language links generated from stored article addresses are each small, contained pieces of work. Together they decide whether search engines see 611 disconnected pages or a body of work they can attribute, date and rank.

7. Nothing on the Site Proves You Are a Real Company

Critical  We approached the site the way a cautious buyer would, and tried to answer a simple question: who is this?

The site does not say. There is no About page. No company name appears anywhere in the page text, no registered address, no company number, and no named people. There are no author names on any of the 607 articles. The privacy policy and terms of service links in the footer are placeholders that lead nowhere: their destination in the code is href="#", and the corresponding pages return "page not found".

The social media icons in the footer are also unedited placeholders, pointing at https://twitter.com and https://linkedin.com, the platforms' own home pages, rather than at any profile.

This last one is a missed opportunity as much as an error, because a real company profile does exist. We found a LinkedIn page under the name "AI Token King (ATK)", listing 11 to 50 employees. The site does not link to it. Its follower count is 5.

Two external factors sharpen this. The domain was registered on 9 April 2026, making it about four months old, and it has never been captured by the Internet Archive. This is the cheapest large win available. An About page naming the company and the people, a real privacy policy and terms, a contact route, author names on articles, and a correct link to the LinkedIn profile are perhaps two days of combined writing and development. They move the trustworthiness score more than any other single action, and several of them are prerequisites for enterprise procurement.

8. The Phone Experience Hides the One Thing You Want Clicked

High  The mobile layout is technically sound: at 390 pixels wide no page overflows sideways, every page has a clear headline near the top, and there are no broken images or script errors. The problem is what the first screen prioritises. The primary conversion button, "Get Started", is not visible on the phone at all, sitting inside the closed navigation menu, so reaching it takes two taps. The cookie consent panel meanwhile occupies 27.5 percent of the first screen with no close button, and on six of the thirteen pages tested it is the largest visible element.

Separately, the blog index is extremely heavy: 18,636 kilobytes on desktop, of which 18,184 are images, being 146 full-resolution files none of them resized for display. Because no image declares its dimensions the page also shifts as it loads, scoring 0.71 on Google's layout stability measure where anything above 0.25 is poor. Sitewide, 100 of 104 images are served at more than twice their displayed size, the worst being 1,344 by 768 pixel files shown in an 80 by 80 pixel box.

What this means.

Three contained fixes. Put a "Get Started" button in the visible header bar on mobile rather than inside the menu. Add width and height attributes to images and serve them resized through the image service already in use, which will cut the blog index from roughly 18 megabytes to under 2 and fix the layout shifting. Give the consent panel a smaller footprint and a working privacy link.

9. Risks That Need a Decision, Not Just a Fix

The items in this chapter carry legal, regulatory or platform exposure. They are separated out because they need a decision from you or your advisers, not only a developer ticket.

Risk.

Analytics run in four languages with no privacy policy. Google Analytics (property G-2KG5EVJQ22) and Cloudflare's analytics are both live on every page. The site has no privacy policy, and the consent panel's own link to that policy is a placeholder. The site publishes in Spanish, so European visitors are likely. To the site's credit, the consent panel does hold analytics until the visitor accepts, which is the harder half of the requirement. Publishing a genuine privacy policy and wiring the link to it closes the gap.

Risk.

The commercial relationship behind the calls to action is not disclosed. Every conversion path on the site leaves for aitokenking.com.tw. The code shipped by that site indicates it operates as a white-labelled tenant of a platform trading as BasicRouter. We found no disclosure of any commercial or affiliate relationship anywhere on aitoken.global: a search of the codebase for the terms affiliate, disclosure, sponsored, commission and referral returns no matches. Whether a disclosure is legally required depends on the commercial terms, which we cannot see. This should be confirmed with counsel rather than assumed either way.

Risk.

An enterprise compliance offer with no identifiable seller. The compliance page markets governance and audit services to financial institutions and listed companies. It names no legal entity, provides no contact route, and its enquiry buttons link to themselves. Selling compliance services while displaying no verifiable corporate identity is a credibility problem in the one market segment least likely to tolerate it.

Risk.

Placeholder links are live in production. The footer social icons point at twitter.com and linkedin.com rather than at your profiles, and two legal links point nowhere. These are visible to every visitor and read as an unfinished site.

Worth watching.

Cached pages could persist for a year. Pages are cached at the edge network with a one-year lifetime. Content updates depend on that cache being cleared at each deploy. We confirmed the live pages were fresher than the last commit on the main branch, so a rebuild path clearly works, but we could not verify the cache-clearing mechanism itself. If it is ever missed, a stale price could remain published for a very long time.

11. What Is Already Good

An audit that lists only faults is a sales pitch rather than an assessment. The following are genuine strengths, verified in the same way as the problems.

The engineering foundation is sound

  • Pages are served as complete HTML from a global network. Google's crawler, OpenAI's crawler and an ordinary browser all receive byte-for-byte identical content. Many modern sites fail this test badly; this one passes cleanly.
  • Modern transport is fully configured, with compression reducing the home page from 101 kilobytes to 17.5 kilobytes.
  • Invalid addresses return proper "not found" responses, and addresses without a trailing slash redirect correctly and permanently.
  • Content pages other than the blog index are lean, at 419 to 424 kilobytes.

The front end is carefully built

  • Zero console errors, zero failed resources and zero broken images across all thirteen pages tested.
  • No horizontal overflow at 390 pixels on any page, and a unique headline visible near the top of all 26 desktop and mobile captures.
  • A visible keyboard focus indicator on all 312 interactive elements tested, which many commercial sites lack entirely.
  • The consent panel presents accept and reject with equal prominence and genuinely withholds analytics until the visitor chooses. That is the ethically and legally harder half done correctly.

The content operation has already improved

  • The 33 articles published in July and August 2026 have a median length of 1,878 words, cite their sources, and are transparent about method. The editorial process that produced them works. The problem is the older material, not the current capability.
  • Translation quality is high across Spanish, Indonesian and Vietnamese, with no source-language text left in the body copy. Vietnamese articles are the deepest on the site, averaging 1,371 words.
  • Metadata is genuinely translated rather than copied: across 399 comparable pairs there were zero identical descriptions and zero identical headlines.
  • There are zero duplicate meta descriptions across all 656 pages, which is unusual at this scale.
  • The token calculator carries honest disclaimers about estimate accuracy.
  • Analytics event tracking is already wired to the calls to action, so measurement will work as soon as there is something to measure.

Method

Ground truth was established first by direct HTTP requests, then five specialist reviews ran in parallel, each briefed with that shared ground truth. Every finding rated Critical was independently re-verified by the lead auditor against the live site before inclusion here, and three claims were corrected as a result. Coverage was complete rather than sampled: all 656 published addresses were fetched and parsed, and all 1,257 unique language-link targets were requested individually.

Annotation boxes were positioned from measured element coordinates taken from the rendered page, not drawn by eye. Chart colours were checked with a contrast and colour-blindness validator against the white page background: the two-colour comparison passes all six categorical checks, and the severity scale passes all four ordinal checks.

Conflicts reconciled

Two reviews reported different rates of language-link breakage, 59.9 percent and 57.7 percent. Both were set aside in favour of a direct count from the raw dataset, which gives 1,511 broken annotations out of 3,275, or 46.1 percent. The broken count itself was consistent across sources; only the denominators differed. This report therefore uses the per-page figure, 603 of 656 pages affected, which all three sources support and which is the more meaningful measure.

Two expectations carried into the audit were not supported by the evidence and are recorded as negative findings: duplicate page titles and descriptions are not a significant problem here, and translated metadata is genuine rather than copied. A third, the common pattern of a site rendered only in the browser and invisible to crawlers, was tested for and found absent.

Verification limits

The following could not be verified and are recorded as no data rather than estimated. Which hostname Google actually has indexed, because a domain-level property masks the distinction. The cache-clearing mechanism used at deploy. Whether the hosting is managed or raw content delivery network configuration. Per-page indexation status, traffic and Core Web Vitals from real users, all of which require account access. Backlink volume, which requires a paid tool. Screen reader behaviour, tested only through structural inspection. Performance under throttled network conditions. Intermediate screen widths between 390 and 1,920 pixels. Non-English pages were not covered by the interface review. Several social and review platforms return blocking responses to automated requests, specifically Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, Threads, Medium, Reddit, Trustpilot, G2, Capterra and Product Hunt; absence of a profile on those platforms is therefore unverified rather than confirmed. LinkedIn absences were control-tested against a known-good profile and are asserted as verified. Company register searches returned an entity whose name does not match the one implied by the product code, and that discrepancy remains unresolved. Whether the addresses found are mass virtual offices is suspected but not established.
